What Is Contract Furniture?

The term appears on every commercial furniture website and almost nobody explains it. Here's what contract furniture actually means, the standards behind it, and when you genuinely need it.

The short answer

Contract furniture is furniture designed, built and tested for commercial use — offices, hotels, schools, bars, care homes, anywhere the public or a workforce will use it day in, day out. The name comes from how it's bought: historically through a commercial contract for a project or business, rather than off the shelf by a household. The label isn't about looks. Plenty of contract furniture is indistinguishable from what you'd put in your living room. The difference is what it has to survive, and what it has to prove.

A domestic chair might be sat on for a couple of hours a day by the same person. A chair in a busy office, café or student common room takes constant use from hundreds of different people, none of whom own it and few of whom are gentle with it. Contract furniture is engineered — and certified — for that life.

What makes furniture "contract grade"

There's no single badge that says "contract". Instead, it's a cluster of British and European standards that commercial furniture is tested against. The ones that come up most often in UK specifications:

  • BS EN 16139 — strength, durability and safety requirements for non-domestic seating. This is the core "will it survive commercial use" test for chairs.
  • BS EN 527 — the equivalent for office desks and tables, covering dimensions, stability and strength.
  • BS 5459-2 — the heavy-duty office chair standard, for task seating used up to 24 hours a day and by users up to 150 kg. Relevant for control rooms and shift-work environments.
  • BS 7176 — ignition resistance for upholstery in non-domestic settings. This is the fire-safety side, and it's where ratings like Crib 5 come in — we've covered that in detail in our Crib 5 guide.

Alongside the test standards, contract furniture is normally sold with a commercial-use warranty — typically five years or more, sometimes ten. That matters because the reverse is also true: domestic furniture warranties almost always exclude commercial use. Put a consumer sofa in your reception and the day it fails, the warranty usually isn't worth the paper it's printed on.

Do you actually need it?

For anything used daily in a workplace or public setting — task chairs, desks, meeting furniture, reception seating, café and breakout furniture — the honest answer is yes. Not because a rule says a private business can't buy its chairs from a retail park, but because of three practical realities:

  • Lifespan. Domestic furniture in commercial use tends to fail in months rather than years — and replacing furniture twice costs more than buying contract-grade once.
  • Liability and insurance. If an employee or visitor is injured by a failed chair, being able to show the furniture was fit for commercial use matters. In regulated environments — hospitality, student accommodation, care — fire ratings on upholstery aren't optional at all.
  • Warranty. As above: commercial use voids most domestic warranties on day one.

Where you can relax a little is genuinely light-use furniture — the decorative armchair in a corner nobody sits in. But as a rule of thumb: if it's used daily by people who don't own it, buy contract.

Suppliers vs manufacturers — how the UK market actually works

A lot of buyers search for "contract furniture manufacturers" when what they actually need is a supplier — and the distinction is worth understanding, because it changes how you buy.

Most UK and European contract furniture manufacturers don't sell directly to end customers. They sell through trade suppliers and dealers, who hold the trade accounts, handle specification, consolidate deliveries from multiple factories and manage installation. That's not a quirk — it's how the industry is structured, because a real project almost never comes from one factory. A single office refit might involve task seating from one manufacturer, desking from a second, soft seating from a third and tables from a fourth.

A good supplier is brand-agnostic across those factories: the job is to match your budget, lead time and specification to the right maker for each piece, not to push one catalogue. Does quantity matter?

A persistent myth is that contract furniture is only for big projects — that suppliers won't take an order for less than a floor's worth of desks. Some won't. We will: a single desk and chair for a new starter goes through the same trade accounts, at the same trade pricing, as a whole building. If you need one contract-grade chair with the right certification and a proper warranty, that's a legitimate order, not a favour.

What to ask for when you buy

Whoever you buy from, three questions sort serious suppliers from box-shifters: Which standards has this piece been tested to, and can you send the certificates? What is the commercial warranty, in years, in writing? And what is the real lead time to my postcode, delivered and installed? If any of those produce a vague answer, keep looking.
