How to Specify Student Accommodation Furniture

A practical guide for estates teams refurbishing halls: build quality, fire ratings, dimensions, durability, and the ten-year economics that actually matter.

Start with the use-case, not the product list

A student bedroom is one of the most demanding environments for furniture in the UK. It's a small space, occupied for nine months of intensive use, cleaned and turned around annually, inspected quarterly, and expected to survive freshers-week enthusiasm without structural failure. The furniture sees higher concentrated wear than a hotel bedroom and has a longer expected life than office desking. Any specification starts with acknowledging that.

That changes what "good" looks like. A chair that would be fine in a private home will fail inside two academic years. A bed frame that meets general contract rating may still rattle itself apart from nightly use over five years. The specification has to be chosen for the environment, not for aesthetic or pricing parity with other sectors.

Fire compliance: Crib 5 is the floor, not the ceiling

Under BS 7176, student accommodation falls into the "high hazard" category. That means upholstered items (bed bases, headboards, desk chairs where upholstered, any soft seating in communal spaces) need to pass BS 5852 Crib 5 at the composite level. Case goods (wardrobes, desks, drawer units) don't generally need Crib 5 as there's no upholstery, but they still need appropriate fire performance for visible surfaces and any foamed components.

If you're working with a specifier or architect on a new build or refurb, insist on composite-level certification at quotation stage. "The fabric is Crib 5" without the matching foam certification is not enough for an insurance audit. For the full picture on what Crib 5 tests and doesn't test, see our Crib 5 explainer.

Build quality: what to actually look for

Below the headline rating, the details that drive ten-year life are:

  • Case goods edging. 2mm ABS or PUR edging, not 0.5mm paper foil. Paper foil peels within 18 months in a washed-down environment.
  • Drawer runners. Full-extension metal runners rated for 25kg+ cycles of 80,000+. Plastic runners and short drop-on mechanisms fail.
  • Bed frame construction. Steel or solid-timber frames, not MDF or chipboard where the bolts enter the material. Bolt-through fixings that can be tightened on re-assembly.
  • Chair frames. Welded steel or bent-ply frames with tested cycle counts. Stackable operator chairs with four-arm spiders do not last.
  • Mattress. Contract-rated foam density (35kg/m³ minimum), waterproof washable cover, and BS 7177-compliant fire rating.

Dimensions that matter

Most halls bedrooms are 11–13m² with a standard 2m×0.9m single bed. The spec details that come up most often:

  • Wardrobe width of 600mm or 700mm: 700mm fits a typical student's hanging load plus a shelf stack
  • Desk depth of 600mm (minimum for a laptop plus notebook) and a width of 1100–1200mm
  • Bed under-bed clearance of 300mm to take two standard under-bed storage boxes
  • Chair seat height 440–460mm for comfortable fit to a 720mm desk

The ten-year economics

A 300-room hall refurb might have a capital budget of £2,000 per bedroom on furniture. The temptation is to chase the lowest specification that meets the fire paperwork. The risk is that, of the fifteen items in a bedroom, two or three will fail in years three to five, and the replacement cost (the product plus the labour, the room being taken out of income, the co-ordination with the estates team) is usually 2x the original unit price. Over a ten-year cycle, a £2,200 specification that holds is typically cheaper than a £1,900 specification that doesn't.

This is the conversation we'd rather have up-front than after the first summer turnaround when the estates team is replacing desk chairs for the second time.

Summer delivery windows

Student accommodation refurb runs on an immovable calendar: students out mid-June, students in early September. That's a ten-week delivery-and-install window for work that is often hundreds of rooms across multiple blocks. Plan backwards from handover: install starts week of handover minus four; delivery arrives week of handover minus five; order placed no later than week of handover minus fifteen for any built-to-order items.
